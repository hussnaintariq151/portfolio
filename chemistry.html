How I turn lab data into models

Every point on a plot cost somebody a day in the lab. That is the difference between scientific data and the kind most models are trained on — it is expensive, there is never enough of it, and it arrives with an error bar attached. Each pillar below opens into the practices it covers; open any one of those for what it actually is.

01 Measurement & uncertainty a number without its error is a rumour
Error propagation

Carrying instrument and replicate error through every calculation, so the final figure arrives with a range rather than a false precision.

Replicates & controls

Running the same condition more than once, with a known control beside it, so a difference can be told apart from noise.

Detection limits

Knowing the value below which the instrument is reporting its own noise, and refusing to model anything underneath it.

Calibration curves

Relating instrument signal to real concentration through a fitted standard series, and re-running it when the instrument drifts.

Significant figures

Reporting only the digits the measurement supports. A model that returns six of them is not more accurate, only less honest.

02 Experimental data & provenance what the instrument actually gave you
Instrument exports

Getting data out of whatever format the instrument writes, without losing the metadata attached to it on the way.

Run & batch metadata

Recording which instrument, which operator, which day, which batch — because those are the confounders that surface later.

Unit normalisation

Putting everything into one unit system before anything is compared. A large share of scientific data bugs are unit bugs.

Outlier triage

Deciding whether a strange point is a failed measurement or a real result by going back to the run, not by reaching for a statistical rule.

Dataset versioning

Pinning the exact dataset behind a figure, so a result from six months ago can be reproduced rather than approximately recalled.

03 Molecular & materials representation turning matter into features
SMILES & molecular graphs

Representing a molecule as a string or a graph a model can read, canonicalised so the same molecule has exactly one form.

Molecular descriptors

Computed physicochemical properties — mass, logP, polar surface area, atom counts — as the tabular features a classical model needs.

Fingerprints

Encoding which substructures are present as a bit vector, which is what similarity search and most screening models actually run on.

Composition & structure features

For materials: stoichiometry, crystal structure and processing conditions turned into columns rather than left in the method section.

Standardisation

Salts stripped, tautomers and charge states resolved, so two records of one compound do not enter the dataset as two compounds.

04 Feature engineering from chemistry domain knowledge as columns
Physically meaningful features

Building the features the underlying chemistry says should matter, rather than throwing every computable descriptor at the model and hoping.

Derived rates & slopes

Turning a time series into the quantity that carries the signal — a capacity fade slope, a rate constant, a degradation rate.

Condition encoding

Temperature, C-rate, pH, pressure and solvent captured as features, because a result only means anything alongside the conditions that produced it.

Correlated feature pruning

Removing descriptors that measure the same thing, so importance stays readable and the model is not counting one property five times.

05 Validation for scientific data splits that don’t lie
Scaffold & cluster splits

Splitting by molecular scaffold or material family rather than at random, so the test set is genuinely new chemistry instead of near-duplicates.

Grouped splits

Keeping every measurement from one batch, cell or plate on the same side of the split, so a batch effect cannot leak the answer across.

Temporal splits

Training on earlier experiments and testing on later ones, because that is the order in which the model will actually be used.

Applicability domain

Stating where the model is allowed to be trusted, and having it decline rather than extrapolate outside the chemistry it was shown.

Uncertainty on predictions

Every prediction carrying a confidence, so a screening list can be ordered by expected value rather than by point estimate alone.

06 Modelling & screening from dataset to a shortlist
Baselines from chemistry

A physical or rule-based estimate first, because a model that cannot beat a known correlation has not added anything yet.

Property prediction

Regression or classification on a measured property — solubility, capacity, activity — with the error bar carried through to the output.

Virtual screening

Ranking a large candidate library so the lab tests the top of a list instead of a random slice of it.

Small-data methods

Techniques that work at a few hundred points, because that is what a real lab produces — regularisation, priors, and transfer from related tasks.

Interpretability

Being able to point at which descriptors drove a prediction, so a chemist can agree with it or argue with it.

07 Active learning & experiment design which experiment to run next
Acquisition strategy

Choosing the next experiments by expected information gain rather than by best predicted value, so the model keeps learning instead of confirming itself.

Exploration vs exploitation

Balancing candidates that look good against candidates the model is unsure about — deliberately, with a stated ratio, not by accident.

Design of experiments

Varying conditions on a designed grid instead of one factor at a time, so interactions become visible within the same number of runs.

Closing the loop

Feeding measured results back as labelled data, so every round of experiments makes the next round of predictions better.

08 Reproducibility & reporting a result somebody else can get
Notebook to pipeline

Moving analysis out of an ad-hoc notebook into a script that runs the same way twice, in the same order, from the same inputs.

Pinned environments

Fixed dependency versions and fixed seeds, so a rerun produces the same numbers on a different machine a year later.

Figures from code

Every plot regenerated from the data by a script, so a figure and the numbers behind it cannot quietly drift apart.

Reporting negatives

Recording what did not work. In R&D that is half the information, and it is almost never written down.

Audit trail

Who changed what and when, so a result that ends up in a decision can be traced back to the run that produced it.

Read this as the standard, not the résumé: it is the list I hold scientific work to. Large-scale quantum chemistry, automated high-throughput experimentation, and GLP or GMP-regulated pipelines I have not run — I know the shapes and the trade-offs, and I would rather say so plainly than claim scars I do not have.

Subjects

01 Electrochemistry charge crossing an interface
Redox reactions

Reactions where electrons transfer between species — one oxidised, one reduced. The electron count is what makes the equation balance.

Nernst equation

The relationship between a cell’s voltage and the concentrations inside it, which is why a battery’s voltage falls as it discharges.

Electrode kinetics

How fast charge actually crosses the electrode surface, described by overpotential and exchange current rather than by thermodynamics alone.

Cyclic voltammetry

Sweeping the potential back and forth while recording current, so redox processes appear as peaks whose shape says how reversible they are.

Impedance spectroscopy

A small AC signal applied across frequencies, separating resistance, charge transfer and diffusion — which a single DC measurement cannot tell apart.

Electrolytes

The ion-conducting medium between electrodes. Its stability window sets the voltage a cell can reach before the electrolyte starts decomposing itself.

02 Thermodynamics what a reaction is allowed to do
Gibbs free energy

Whether a reaction can happen at all, combining enthalpy and entropy at a temperature. Negative means spontaneous — and says nothing about how fast.

Enthalpy & entropy

Heat exchanged, and how many ways a system can arrange itself. Together they set direction; separately they explain why heating can reverse it.

Phase equilibria

Which phases coexist at a given temperature and pressure, and where the boundaries sit — the map behind every phase diagram.

Equilibrium constants

The ratio of products to reactants a reaction settles at, and how that ratio shifts when concentration, pressure or temperature changes.

Electrochemical potential

Free energy per particle including the electrical term — what actually drives ions across a membrane or an electrode interface.

03 Chemical kinetics how fast, and by what route
Rate laws

How reaction rate depends on concentration. Determined by experiment, never read off the balanced equation.

Reaction order

The exponent on each concentration in the rate law, which points at how many species take part in the slow step.

Arrhenius behaviour

Rate against temperature, giving the activation energy — the barrier a reaction has to clear before it can proceed at all.

Reaction mechanisms

The sequence of elementary steps behind one overall equation, and which of those steps is rate-determining.

Catalytic cycles

The loop a catalyst travels, regenerating itself each turn — and the step where it gets stuck when the cycle slows down.

04 Crystallography where the atoms sit
Unit cells & lattices

The smallest repeating box that generates the whole crystal, described by its edge lengths and the angles between them.

Space groups

The full set of symmetry operations a crystal obeys, which constrains what its diffraction pattern is allowed to look like.

X-ray diffraction

Firing X-rays at a crystal and reading the angles they scatter at, because those angles encode the spacing between planes of atoms.

Bragg’s law

The condition for constructive interference in diffraction, which converts a measured angle directly into a lattice spacing.

Rietveld refinement

Fitting a whole calculated pattern to the measured one, so phase fractions and lattice parameters come out with uncertainties attached.

05 Solid-state chemistry structure to property
Crystal defects

Vacancies, interstitials and dislocations. In a battery electrode these are not flaws — they are frequently the paths the ions actually move along.

Band structure

How electron energy levels spread into bands in a solid, which is the difference between a metal, a semiconductor and an insulator.

Ionic conductivity

How readily ions migrate through a solid, which decides whether a material can serve as a solid electrolyte at all.

Solid solutions & doping

Substituting one element for another in a lattice to tune conductivity, stability or capacity without changing the underlying structure.

Intercalation

Ions inserting reversibly between layers of a host structure without breaking it — the mechanism a lithium-ion cathode runs on.

06 Surface chemistry where two phases meet
Adsorption isotherms

How much of a species sticks to a surface at a given pressure or concentration. Langmuir and BET describe the common cases.

Surface area & porosity

Measured area per gram and the pore size distribution behind it, which sets how much of a catalyst or sorbent is actually reachable.

Active sites

The specific locations on a surface where reaction happens — usually a small fraction of the total area, and the fraction that matters.

Catalyst poisoning

A species binding to active sites strongly enough to block them, which is why a catalyst that worked yesterday may not work today.

Interfacial layers

Films that form where electrode meets electrolyte. The SEI in a lithium cell both protects the cell and slowly consumes it.

07 Quantum chemistry electrons, calculated
Schrödinger equation

The equation whose solutions describe the electrons in a molecule. Solved exactly for hydrogen, approximated for everything else.

Molecular orbitals

Atomic orbitals combined into orbitals spanning the whole molecule, which is where bonding, antibonding and reactivity come from.

Density functional theory

Computing electronic structure from electron density rather than the full wavefunction — the approximation that makes real molecules tractable.

Basis sets

The functions used to build the orbitals. Larger is more accurate and much slower, and the choice has to be reported alongside the result.

Computed properties

Geometries, energies, vibrational frequencies and reaction barriers produced by calculation — and then checked against measurement.

08 Spectroscopy light as a measuring instrument
UV-Vis

Absorption of ultraviolet and visible light by electronic transitions, and the concentration measurement the Beer-Lambert relation gets from it.

Infrared & Raman

Vibrational spectroscopy: which bonds are present, and how their environment has changed, read from where the peaks sit and how they shift.

NMR

Nuclear magnetic resonance — atomic connectivity and chemical environment, and the standard way a molecular structure gets confirmed.

Mass spectrometry

Separating ions by mass-to-charge for molecular weight, and reading the fragments for structural information.

XPS

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy: which elements are at a surface and in what oxidation state — in the top few nanometres only.

09 Analytical chemistry getting a number you can trust
Chromatography

Separating a mixture by how strongly each component interacts with a stationary phase, so each can be measured on its own.

Titration

Adding a reagent of known concentration until the reaction completes, which turns a measured volume into a concentration.

Calibration & standards

Relating instrument signal to real quantity through samples of known value, and re-running it as the instrument drifts.

Detection & quantification limits

The smallest amount that can be seen, and the smallest that can be reported as a number. Those are two different thresholds.

Sample preparation

Everything done to a sample before it reaches the instrument. Most analytical error is created here rather than during the measurement itself.

10 Catalysis a cheaper route to the same product
Homogeneous catalysis

Catalyst in the same phase as the reactants. Highly selective and well understood, and awkward to separate from the product afterwards.

Heterogeneous catalysis

Solid catalyst, fluid reactants. Easy to separate and reuse, which is why industrial processes are built on it.

Turnover number & frequency

How many times one active site converts substrate, and how quickly — the two numbers that make different catalysts comparable.

Selectivity

Which product a catalyst favours among the ones it could make. Often worth more than raw activity.

Deactivation

How a catalyst loses performance — sintering, coking, poisoning — and how much of that can be recovered.
